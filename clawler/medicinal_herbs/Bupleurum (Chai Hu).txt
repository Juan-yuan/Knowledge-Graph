Cultivation and Processing of Bupleurum (Chai Hu)
Source
This item is the dried root of Umbelliferae plants Bupleurum chinense DC. (Bupleurum (Chai Hu)) or narrow‑leaved Bupleurum Bupleurum scorzonerifolium Willd. (Narrow‑leaved Bupleurum (Xia Ye Chai Hu)). According to differing characteristics they are commonly called northern Bupleurum and southern Bupleurum. Harvested in spring and autumn, remove stems, leaves and mud and sand, then dry.
Properties and Flavor
Bitter, slightly cold.
Processing
1. Bupleurum (Chai Hu): remove impurities, remove residual stems, wash clean of mud and sand, lift out, moisten thoroughly then promptly slice, and immediately sun‑dry.
2. Vinegar‑processed Bupleurum: take Bupleurum slices, mix evenly with vinegar, place in a pot and stir‑fry over low heat until the vinegar is absorbed and slightly dry, remove and sun‑dry. (For every 100 jin of Bupleurum, use 12 jin of vinegar.)
3. Turtle‑blood Bupleurum: take Bupleurum slices, place in a large basin, pour in turtle blood slightly diluted with warm water, mix evenly, macerate until moistened, place in a pot and lightly stir‑fry over low heat, remove and cool. (For every 100 jin of Bupleurum, use blood from 200 live turtles.)
4. Wine‑processed Bupleurum: take Bupleurum slices, mix evenly with yellow wine, macerate until thoroughly moistened, place in a pot and heat over low fire to stir‑fry until dry, remove and cool. (For every 100 kg of Bupleurum slices, use 10 kg of yellow wine.)
5. Charred Bupleurum: take Bupleurum slices, stir‑fry over low heat until the exterior is black and the interior brownish, spray in a small amount of water, remove and air‑dry.
6. Honey‑processed Bupleurum: put honey into a pot, heat until boiling, pour in Bupleurum slices, stir‑fry over low heat until deep yellow and not sticky to the touch.
Description
Northern Bupleurum (Bei Chai Hu): cylindrical or long conical in shape, length 6–15 cm, diameter 0.3–0.8 cm. Root crown swollen, apex retaining 3–15 stem bases or short fibrous leaf bases, branches in the lower part. Surface dark brown or light brown, with longitudinal wrinkles, lateral root scars and lenticels. Texture hard and tough, not easy to break; fracture shows fibrous nature, cortex light brown, wood portion yellowish‑white. Slight aromatic odor, slightly bitter taste.
Southern Bupleurum (Nan Chai Hu): roots thinner, conical, apex with many fine hair‑like withered leaf fibers, lower part mostly unbranched or slightly branched. Surface reddish‑brown or dark brown, near the root crown often with dense annular rings. Texture somewhat soft, easy to break, fracture surface slightly flat, not showing fibrous nature. Has rancid oily odor.
Effects of Bupleurum (Chai Hu)
Actions
This product is the dried root of Bupleurum or narrow‑leaved Bupleurum (Umbelliferae).
Indications
Mainly for alternating chills and fever, chest fullness and hypochondriac pain, bitter taste in the mouth and deafness, headache and vertigo, malaria, diarrhea and rectal prolapse, irregular menses, uterine prolapse.
Channels
Enter
Liver Channel,
Gallbladder Channel.
Indications
Harmonizes the exterior and interior, spreads the Liver, raises yang. Treats alternating chills and fever, chest fullness and hypochondriac pain, bitter taste and deafness, headache and vertigo, malaria, diarrhea and rectal prolapse, irregular menses, uterine prolapse.
① Ben Jing: Governs qi accumulation in the heart, abdomen and stomach, food stagnation, pathogenic cold and heat, promotes discharge of the old and generation of the new.
② Bie Lu: Dispels vexation and heat below the heart from cold damage, resolves various phlegm‑heat accumulations, counterflow in the chest, wandering qi among the five viscera, large intestine stagnation, edema, and damp‑bi with cramping. Can also be used as a bathing decoction.
③ Yao Xing Lun: Treats summer‑heat and bone‑and‑joint vexing pain, heat qi, shoulder and back pain, promotes circulation of blood and qi, fatigue and emaciation; promotes descending qi and digestion, indicated for epidemic febrile diseases unresolved internally or externally, decocted alone.
④ Qian Jin Fang: Sprout juice treats deafness, used to irrigate the ear.
⑤ Si Sheng Ben Cao: Indicated for copious phlegm and for masses and fullness in the chest and hypochondria.
⑥ Ri Hua Zi Ben Cao: Tonifies the five fatigues and seven injuries, relieves vexation and fright, augments qi and strength, transforms phlegm and stops cough, moistens heart and lungs, replenishes essence and marrow, for febrile diseases with heat, mania and collapse, chest and hypochondrial fullness, and forgetfulness.
⑦ Zhen Zhu Nang: Eliminates alternating chills and fever and gallbladder bi; only Bupleurum shoots can remove it.
⑧ Yi Xue Qi Yuan: Eliminates deficiency vexation and heat, disperses muscular heat, removes morning tidal fever.
⑨ Dian Nan Ben Cao: An essential drug for inducing sweat to release the exterior in cold damage, clears pathogenic heat circulating in the six channels, treats paralysis and atrophy, removes pathogenic heat of the Liver system and consumptive fever, moves constrained Liver‑channel qi, relieves left hypochondriac pain from Liver qi, treats women’s blood‑heat burning during menses and regulates menses. Use young flower buds for inducing sweat; use the root to treat deficiency heat and regulate menses.
⑩ Ben Cao Gang Mu (Compendium of Materia Medica): Treats sinking yang qi, pacifies Liver, Gallbladder, Sanjiao and pericardial fire, and headaches, vertigo, dim or red painful eyes with obstructions, deafness and tinnitus, various malarias, alternating cold and heat from phlegmatic dampness, women’s heat entering the blood chamber and irregular menses, residual heat after pediatric smallpox, and wasting disorders.
Usage and Dosage
Internal: decoction, 3–10 g; or in pills or powders.
External: appropriate amount, decoct for washing; or grind to powder and apply as a poultice.
Precautions and Contraindications
Contraindicated in true yin deficiency and ascendant Liver yang.
① Ben Cao Jing Ji Zhu: Pinellia (Ban Xia) acts as its assistant. Antagonistic to Gleditsia (Zao Jiao). Avoid with Inula (Nu Wan) and Veratrum (Li Lu).
② Yi Xue Ru Men: In cases of exhausted original qi or yin deficit with profuse sweating, mistaken ingestion can be fatal.
③ Ben Cao Jing Shu: Contraindicated in patients who are deficient with rebellious qi rising, in vomiting, and in yin deficiency with blazing fire ascending; same as the classical rules. Do not take for malaria that is not of the Shaoyang channel.

[Notes]
(1) For treating colds it is often used together with Kudzu Root (Ge Gen), Notopterygium (Qiang Huo), and others. Bupleurum (Chai Hu) has a relatively good antipyretic effect; when the pathogen is in the Shaoyang and chills and fever alternate, it is often combined with Scutellaria (Huang Qin) and Pinellia (Ban Xia) (for example, Xiao Chai Hu Tang); for malarial disorders Bupleurum (Chai Hu) can be paired with Tsaoko (Cao Guo) or Unripe Tangerine Peel (Qing Pi). Bupleurum (Chai Hu) has a good effect in soothing the Liver and relieving constraint and also serves as a guide among liver-dispersing medicines; it is an important drug for treating liver qi stagnation. It can be used for chest and hypochondriac pain whether caused internally by liver constraint or externally by trauma; for menstrual irregularities or dysmenorrhea caused by liver qi stagnation it can be used with Angelica (Dang Gui), White Peony (Bai Shao), Cyperus (Xiang Fu), Curcuma (Yu Jin), and other herbs. Bupleurum (Chai Hu) has an ascending and floating nature; when combined with qi-tonifying herbs such as Codonopsis (Dang Shen) and Astragalus (Huang Qi), it can raise the yang and is used for conditions such as chronic diarrhea with prolapse of the rectum and uterine prolapse due to qi deficiency and sinking.
(2) As a single medicine Bupleurum (Chai Hu) is light, clear, ascending and dispersing, and also has the function of facilitating dissemination. It can release the exterior and reduce fever, soothe the Liver and relieve constraint, and can also be used to raise yang qi. Therefore, clinically it can be used for both excess and deficiency syndromes. Different combinations allow it to exert various effects, for example: paired with Kudzu Root (Ge Gen) and Notopterygium (Qiang Huo) it induces sweating and releases the exterior; paired with Scutellaria (Huang Qin) and Artemisia annua (Qing Hao) it clears heat through the exterior; paired with Dichroa (Chang Shan) and Tsaoko (Cao Guo) it arrests malaria and reduces fever; paired with Cyperus (Xiang Fu) and Curcuma (Yu Jin) it soothes the Liver and relieves constraint; paired with Codonopsis (Dang Shen), Astragalus (Huang Qi), Atractylodes (Bai Zhu), and Cimicifuga (Sheng Ma) it raises and lifts yang qi. However, it is not suitable for those with yin deficiency and lack of fluids or with hyperactive liver yang. Bupleurum (Chai Hu) and Kudzu Root (Ge Gen) have similar light, clear, ascending and dispersing actions and are often used together to release the exterior and reduce fever, but each has its strengths: Bupleurum (Chai Hu) soothes the Liver and relieves constraint and, when combined with tonifying herbs, can raise yang to treat uterine prolapse and rectal prolapse, but it does not generate fluids or quench thirst; Kudzu Root (Ge Gen) generates fluids and relieves thirst, can raise clear yang and is used for watery diarrhea, but it does not have the function of soothing the Liver and relieving constraint.
